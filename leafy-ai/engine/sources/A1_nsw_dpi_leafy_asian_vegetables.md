# Leafy Asian vegetables and their nutrition in hydroponics






Source publication date: April 2011

## Hydroponic systems

Hydroponic systems supply nutrients to crop roots in solution with irrigation water. Nutrient solution may be still or recirculated using a pump, and plant roots may be suspended in solution or contained within a soilless substrate.

Hydroponic systems need to provide plant roots with sufficient nutrients, water and oxygen for good growth.

Hydroponic systems suitable for leafy Asian vegetables include still solution hydroponics, substrate hydroponics with recirculating solution, and nutrient film technique (NFT).

### Still solution hydroponics

Still solution hydroponics is a simple hydroponic system suitable for short-term leafy crops. Plants are supported in a small tube-stock pot and the bottom of the pot is immersed in nutrient solution. The nutrient solution supplies water and nutrients through capillary action.

As the crop uses the solution, the nutrient solution level drops. Good quality water with a low concentration of salts is important because fertiliser salts become more concentrated in the remaining solution as the crop grows.

### Recirculating solution and substrate hydroponics

Recirculating nutrient solution systems require more setup, technology, skill and care than still solution systems.

In substrate culture, plants are grown in a soilless medium. Suitable substrates include coconut coir, perlite and rock wool. The substrate needs adequate water-holding capacity and aeration.

Water and nutrients are applied through irrigation systems and runoff can be collected and pumped back to the crop. A screen filter is important to prevent substrate particles from blocking drippers.

Frequent irrigation and a high percentage of runoff can help prevent the nutrient solution from becoming unbalanced too quickly.

### Nutrient film technique

In NFT, plants are supported in a gently sloping shallow gully and the roots are suspended in a flowing stream of nutrient solution. The solution is collected in a closed tank and pumped back to the top of the gully.

Asian vegetables commercially grown in NFT include Pak Choy, Buk Choy, coriander and bunching onion. Kang Kong and Centella are not suited to NFT because prolific roots can block the flow of nutrient solution.

NFT systems commonly used in Australia consist of PVC channels approximately 10–15 cm wide. Channels are arranged on benches with a slope of approximately 1.5–2.0 degrees to allow nutrient solution to return to the recirculation tank.

A typical flow rate is approximately 1–2 L/minute. Tanks commonly range from 4000 to 10000 L.

### Leafy Asian vegetables in NFT

Thai Basil (variety Siam Queen), bunching onion, coriander, choy sum, mustard cabbage, Pak Choy, En Choy and Tat Soi were produced in an NFT study.

The Huett's lettuce formulation was used to prepare the nutrient solution.

Production of these vegetables was suitable at nutrient solution concentrations between 1.5–2.5 dS/m. Mustard Cabbage preferred a higher concentration range of 2.5–3.5 dS/m.

The study reported that Basil, Choy Sum, Tat Soi, Coriander and three varieties of Pak Choy grew best when the nutrient solution was managed at an EC range of 1.5–2.5 dS/m.

## Preparing the nutrient solution

Plants require macronutrients including nitrogen, potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus and sulphur, as well as micronutrients including chlorine, iron, boron, manganese, zinc, copper, nickel and molybdenum.

Prepared hydroponic fertiliser solutions can be used by growers. Calcium should be approximately equal to the amount of nitrogen, or at least 70% of the nitrogen amount. Magnesium should be approximately 20–30% of the amount of calcium.

Fertilisers containing urea should be avoided, as should fertilisers with more than 10% of total nitrogen in ammonium form.

Growers may prepare concentrated stock solutions before diluting them for delivery to the crop.

Two stock solutions, labelled A and B, are used to avoid precipitation of calcium phosphate, calcium sulphate and iron phosphate in concentrated solutions.

Stock solutions may be approximately 100–200 times stronger than the working solution delivered to plants.

## Managing nutrient solution

Nutrients reach the root surface through processes associated with transpiration and concentration gradients created as nutrients are removed by roots.

A recirculating hydroponic solution needs careful management because water and nutrients are continuously taken up by the crop. Hydroponic systems have little buffering capacity against changes in the root zone compared with soil systems.

Hot or windy conditions can increase transpiration and cause nutrients to accumulate near roots. This can contribute to osmotic drought and fertiliser toxicity.

Lowering the EC of the nutrient solution during hot and windy conditions can help plants maintain access to water.

Rapid changes in nutrient concentrations around roots can also affect nutrient uptake and may contribute to nutrient toxicity.

## Controlling nutrient solution pH

The pH of hydroponic solutions should generally be maintained between 5.5 and 6.5.

As plants use the nutrient solution, pH can drift. In leafy crops, pH is more likely to drift upwards because plants take up large amounts of nitrate.

Nutrient deficiencies can occur below pH 5 and above pH 7.5 because pH affects nutrient availability.

## Controlling nutrient solution EC

Electrical conductivity (EC) measures the total concentration of chemical elements in the solution.

A stronger solution has a higher EC than a weaker solution.

Common EC units include dS/m and mS/cm. EC indicates overall solution strength but does not indicate the balance of individual nutrients.

Raw water may contain dissolved nutrients that contribute to the EC of the final hydroponic solution. Water quality should therefore be considered when preparing nutrient solutions.

Target EC can vary according to crop, growth stage and climatic conditions.

For the Asian vegetables studied, most crops including Basil grew best at an EC range of 1.5–2.5 dS/m.

## Nutrient solution analysis

Potassium, nitrate and sodium can be useful to monitor in hydroponic crops or nutrient solutions.

Regular nutrient solution analysis can help determine when modification or replacement is required.

Potassium can decrease to undesirable levels and can be monitored using colourimetric test strips, although laboratory analysis is more accurate.

Nitrate concentration can indicate the nitrogen supply available in the nutrient solution.

Sodium is important to monitor in recirculating nutrient solutions when raw water quality is poor. Raw water should contain less than 30 mg/L sodium.

The sodium concentration in nutrient solution should not exceed 150 mg/L for leafy Asian vegetables.

High sodium can be associated with reduced potassium uptake.

## Nutrient disorders

Nutrient deficiency diagnosis can be difficult because symptoms may only become visible after a problem is established.

Symptoms can include reduced growth, chlorosis and necrosis affecting new or old leaves.

Nitrogen deficiency can cause severe stunting and uniformly pale or yellow older leaves. Reddening of older leaves and stems can also occur.

Phosphorus deficiency can cause stunting, small leaves and dull or grey-green or purple colouring of older leaves. A nutrient solution that is too alkaline can contribute to phosphorus deficiency; adjusting the solution to pH 5.5–6.0 is suggested.

Potassium deficiency can cause slight stunting and yellowing or scorching of old leaves at the edges and between leaf veins.

Calcium deficiency can cause scorching of growing tips and margins of new leaves. Leaves may cup as they expand.

Iron deficiency can cause yellowing of young leaves between the leaf veins. An overly alkaline nutrient solution can contribute to iron deficiency.

Magnesium deficiency can cause mild growth reduction and yellowing or scorching of older leaves between veins.

Zinc deficiency can restrict leaf size and stem length and cause yellowing between leaf veins.

Boron deficiency can cause yellowing and scorching between leaf veins at growing points.

Copper deficiency can cause leaves to become dark blue-green.

Sulphur deficiency can cause yellowing and purpling between veins and marginal scorching of older leaves.

Manganese deficiency can cause yellowing between leaf veins progressing to scorching.

## Nutrient toxicity

Excess fertiliser can cause wilting and death of seedlings and growing tips, marginal yellowing and scorching of leaves, and root burning.

High nutrient solution strength can contribute to toxicity. EC should be checked and gradually lowered when required.

Excess phosphorus can result from frequent pH adjustment with phosphoric acid.

High sodium concentrations can also contribute to toxicity.

An excessive supply of micronutrients while attempting to correct a deficiency can also cause toxicity.
