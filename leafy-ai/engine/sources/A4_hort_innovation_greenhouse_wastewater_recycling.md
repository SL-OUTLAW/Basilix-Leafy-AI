# National Greenhouse Waste-Water Recycling Project

## Closed-loop greenhouse water recycling

The National Greenhouse Waste-Water Recycling Project investigated converting greenhouse vegetable production systems from free-drainage systems to full recycling systems.

The project aimed to provide growers with the technical knowledge needed to convert open systems to closed systems.

The project also investigated potential pathology risks associated with recycling greenhouse waste-water.

## Waste-water management

Waste-water from hydroponic greenhouse systems contains water and dissolved nutrients.

Unmanaged discharge can create environmental impacts.

The project identified the need for greenhouse waste-water to be managed on farm.

Recycling nutrient-rich drainage water can reduce the amount of nutrient-containing water discharged into the environment.

## Nutrient recycling

Recycling allows a proportion of nutrients remaining in drainage water to be returned to the crop production system.

Nutrient concentrations change as crops take up water and individual nutrients at different rates.

A recirculating system therefore requires monitoring and adjustment of nutrient inputs to maintain an appropriate nutrient balance.

## Water savings

The project reported that converting greenhouse systems to recycling could substantially reduce water use.

The project reported average savings of approximately 40 percent water under the systems and conditions investigated.

Actual savings depend on system design, crop requirements and management practices.

## Fertiliser savings

Recycling nutrient-rich drainage water can also reduce fertiliser requirements.

The project reported average fertiliser savings of approximately 60 percent under the systems and conditions investigated.

The amount of fertiliser that can be recovered depends on drainage volume, nutrient concentrations and the management of the recirculating system.

## Drainage and root-zone management

The project reported that drainage is important for maintaining suitable root-zone conditions.

The workshop information described an average drainage percentage of approximately 40 percent in the systems investigated.

Drainage percentage and electrical conductivity can be used together to estimate potential nutrient and fertiliser savings.

These values are historical project observations and should not be treated as universal operating targets.

## Sterilisation and pathogen management

Recirculating water can create a pathway for pathogens to move through a production system.

The project investigated the effectiveness of different methods for sterilising recycled water.

Growers were provided with information on how to safely recirculate nutrient-rich waste-water and manage potential disease risks.

Water sterilisation should therefore be considered when designing a closed hydroponic system.

## Nutrient rebalancing

Recycling water does not maintain the original nutrient proportions automatically.

Plants remove different nutrients at different rates.

As a result, nutrient concentrations in recycled solution can become unbalanced.

Monitoring and nutrient rebalancing are required to maintain suitable nutrient conditions.

## Environmental benefits

The project identified potential environmental benefits from reducing discharge of nutrient-rich greenhouse drainage water.

The project estimated that widespread adoption could divert substantial volumes of nutrient-rich water from the environment.

Reducing water and fertiliser losses can also reduce production costs.

## Grower training

The project delivered technical information through theoretical and practical workshops in key Australian growing regions.

A training package was also developed to support adoption of greenhouse water-recycling practices.

The project combined international literature with Australian industry experience to identify knowledge gaps and practical approaches.
