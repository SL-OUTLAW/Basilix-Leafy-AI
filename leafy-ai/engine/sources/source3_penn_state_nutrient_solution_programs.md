# Hydroponics Systems: Nutrient Solution Programs and Recipes






Updated: March 18, 2024

## Nutrient solution approaches

In hydroponic systems, most plant nutrients are supplied through nutrient solutions.

Three basic approaches to creating nutrient solutions are fertilizer programs, nutrient solution recipes, and complete soluble fertilizers.

## Fertilizer programs

Fertilizer programs consist of a complete fertilizer supplemented with additional macronutrients.

These programs can vary according to the hydroponic crop.

An example is a hydroponic tomato program in which a complete fertilizer is supplemented with calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate depending on the variety or growth stage.

### Advantages of fertilizer programs

Fertilizer programs are easy to use.

Only a small number of fertilizers may need to be ordered.

They require little or no mathematical calculation when preparing nutrient solutions.

### Disadvantages of fertilizer programs

Fertilizer programs do not allow easy adjustment of individual nutrients.

They also do not allow growers to account for nutrients already present in the water source.

For example, if the source water already contains potassium, a fixed fertilizer program may not allow the potassium supplied by the fertilizer to be reduced.

Fertilizer programs can also cost more than using nutrient solution recipes.

## Nutrient solution recipes

Nutrient solution recipes specify the amount of each nutrient needed in the solution.

Recipes are generally crop-specific and can be obtained from university Extension services, online sources and trade publications.

An example given by Penn State Extension is a Modified Sonneveld solution recipe for herbs.

The reported nutrient concentrations are:

Nitrogen: 150 ppm

Phosphorus: 31 ppm

Potassium: 210 ppm

Calcium: 90 ppm

Magnesium: 24 ppm

Iron: 1 ppm

Manganese: 0.25 ppm

Zinc: 0.13 ppm

Copper: 0.023 ppm

Boron: 0.16 ppm

Molybdenum: 0.024 ppm

Growers select fertilizers based on the nutrient composition required by the recipe.

Common fertilizer sources include calcium nitrate, ammonium nitrate, potassium nitrate, iron chelate, monopotassium phosphate, magnesium sulfate, borax, sodium molybdate, zinc sulfate, copper sulfate and manganese sulfate.

The amount of fertilizer required is calculated from the amount of the target nutrient contained in the fertilizer and the target nutrient concentration specified by the recipe.

### Advantages of nutrient solution recipes

Recipes allow growers to adjust fertilizer additions based on nutrients already present in the water source.

For example, if the water contains 30 ppm potassium and the recipe requires 210 ppm potassium, the fertilizer needs to provide the remaining 180 ppm potassium.

Recipes also allow individual nutrients to be adjusted when plant analysis indicates a deficiency.

Because individual nutrients can be adjusted, recipes may allow fertilizers to be used more efficiently.

Using recipes can also be less costly than fertilizer programs.

### Disadvantages of nutrient solution recipes

Recipes require calculations to determine how much fertilizer should be added.

A high-precision scale may be required to measure micronutrients because they are needed in small quantities.

## Complete soluble fertilizer approach

Some growers use a complete soluble fertilizer containing micronutrients.

With this approach, nutrients are commonly applied according to the nitrogen requirement of the crop.

For hydroponic lettuce, the article gives an example of approximately 100–150 ppm nitrogen.

### Advantages

Using one complete fertilizer is the simplest of the three approaches.

Only one fertilizer needs to be purchased.

Only one fertilizer injector is needed.

Complete fertilizers are widely available commercially.

### Disadvantages

A complete fertilizer alone may not provide the correct balance of nutrients.

It may also fail to supply adequate amounts of nutrients such as magnesium or calcium if those nutrients are absent or insufficient in the fertilizer.

## Choosing fertilizers

Important factors when selecting fertilizers include solubility, cost, market availability and fertilizer source.

Solubility is the amount of a substance that dissolves in a given amount of solvent.

In hydroponics, irrigation water is the solvent.

Fertilizer solubility varies between fertilizer products and is affected by water temperature.

Fertilizers generally have higher solubility in hot water than cold water.

Greenhouse-grade fertilizers are recommended because they have higher purity and greater solubility than standard-grade fertilizers.

## Nitrogen form

The form of nitrogen supplied can affect plant growth.

Ammonium is used immediately by the plant and is not stored.

Ammonium can promote rapid growth but can also cause excessive vegetative growth, particularly under poor light conditions, and can contribute to ammonium toxicity.

Ammonium toxicity can cause yellowing and death of growing points.

Nitrate is reduced to ammonia before it is metabolised by the plant and can be stored in plant vacuoles.

Compared with ammonium, nitrate tends to slow vegetative growth and may be useful during winter, cold conditions or when slower growth is desired.

## Nutrient availability and chelation

Some nutrients, including iron, manganese and zinc, can become unavailable to plants relatively easily.

Chelates are organic molecules that surround metal nutrients and help keep them available for plant uptake even when pH changes.

## Nutrient solution concentration and compatibility

Nutrient solutions can be supplied at normal strength or as concentrated stock solutions.

Common stock-solution concentrations include 50-, 100- or 200-times normal strength, depending on injector capabilities and fertilizer solubility.

When nutrient solutions are concentrated, some combinations of nutrients can form precipitates that may clog irrigation or drip lines.

A common approach is to use two stock tanks for concentrated nutrient solutions.

Calcium should generally be kept in a different tank from phosphates and sulfates to reduce the risk of precipitation.

## Keeping nutrients in solution

Some fertilizers can be difficult to keep dissolved because of their solubility properties.

Agitation can improve fertilizer solubility.

Large tanks can use propeller-type mixers or circulating pumps.

Smaller tanks can use air pumps for agitation.

Because fertilizers are salts and can corrode some materials, corrosion-resistant materials such as stainless steel should be used for agitation equipment.
