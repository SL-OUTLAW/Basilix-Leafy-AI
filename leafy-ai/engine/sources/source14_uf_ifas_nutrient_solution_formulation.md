# Nutrient Solution Formulation for Hydroponic Production

## Nutrient solution formulation

Hydroponic crops require nutrient solutions containing the essential mineral elements needed for plant growth.

A complete nutrient solution should provide macronutrients, secondary nutrients and micronutrients in appropriate proportions.

Nutrient concentrations should be selected according to the crop, growing system and production conditions.

## Fertilizer materials

Individual fertilizer materials can be combined to produce a nutrient solution.

Common fertilizer materials include calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, monopotassium phosphate, magnesium sulfate and micronutrient sources.

Different fertilizer materials provide different combinations of nutrients.

The nutrient contribution of each fertilizer should be considered when preparing a complete solution.

## Nutrient conversions

Fertilizer analyses may express phosphorus as P2O5 and potassium as K2O rather than elemental phosphorus and potassium.

To convert P2O5 to elemental phosphorus, the P2O5 value can be multiplied by approximately 0.44.

To convert K2O to elemental potassium, the K2O value can be multiplied by approximately 0.83.

These conversions are important when matching fertilizer analysis to a nutrient recipe.

## Stock solutions

Hydroponic fertilizer programs can use concentrated stock solutions that are diluted before reaching the crop.

A stock solution allows concentrated fertilizer materials to be injected into irrigation water.

Stock concentration and injector ratio must be considered when calculating the amount of fertilizer required.

The amount of fertilizer needed depends on the target nutrient concentration, the volume of the stock tank and the dilution ratio.

## Fertilizer compatibility

Some fertilizer components should be kept separate in concentrated stock solutions because they can react and form insoluble compounds.

Calcium-containing fertilizers should generally be kept separate from concentrated sulfate and phosphate fertilizers.

Separating these materials reduces the risk of precipitation.

Precipitates can reduce nutrient availability and may clog irrigation equipment.

## Water quality

The source water used to prepare nutrient solutions contributes its own minerals.

Water quality should therefore be considered when developing a fertilizer program.

Important characteristics can include alkalinity, electrical conductivity and the concentration of calcium, magnesium and other dissolved minerals.

The contribution of nutrients already present in the source water may need to be accounted for when calculating fertilizer additions.

## Nutrient concentration

Nutrient concentrations should be expressed consistently when preparing and evaluating nutrient solutions.

Concentration calculations may use units such as parts per million or milligrams per litre.

One part per million in water is approximately equivalent to one milligram per litre.

Accurate calculations are important when preparing concentrated stock solutions.

## Micronutrients

Micronutrients are required in much smaller concentrations than macronutrients.

Examples include iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and molybdenum.

Micronutrient fertilizer additions may involve very small quantities.

Accurate weighing and careful mixing are therefore important when preparing nutrient solutions.

## Premixed fertilizers

Commercial premixed fertilizers can simplify nutrient-solution preparation.

Premixes may contain several nutrients in predetermined proportions.

When using a premix, the nutrient analysis of the product should be considered together with the nutrient requirements of the crop.

Additional calcium nitrate or other fertilizer materials may be required to balance the final solution.

## Managing nutrient programs

A nutrient program should be evaluated according to the crop's growth stage and the characteristics of the growing system.

Nutrient concentrations may need to change as crop demand changes.

The nutrient solution should be monitored and adjusted when appropriate.

Electrical conductivity provides a general indication of dissolved salts but does not identify the concentration of individual nutrients.
