## Hydroponics

Hydroponics can be defined as growing plants in water containing nutrients or growing plants without soil.

Examples include nutrient film technique (NFT) systems and deep-water float systems where plant roots are placed in nutrient solutions.

Growing plants in soilless media or aggregate media such as sand, gravel and coconut coir can also be considered hydroponic production.

## Essential nutrients

Plants require 17 essential nutrients for normal growth and development.

The macronutrients are carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, calcium and magnesium.

The micronutrients are iron, manganese, zinc, boron, molybdenum, chlorine, copper and nickel.

Macronutrients are required in larger amounts than micronutrients.

Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen are obtained from air and water. In hydroponic systems, the remaining nutrients are supplied through nutrient solutions or aggregate media.

### Nutrient roles

Nitrogen is a component of amino acids, proteins, coenzymes and nucleic acids.

Sulfur is a component of sulfur-containing amino acids, proteins and coenzyme A.

Phosphorus is involved in ATP, NADP intermediates of metabolism, membrane phospholipids and nucleic acids.

Potassium is involved in enzyme activation, turgor and osmotic regulation.

Calcium is involved in enzyme activation, signal transduction and cell structure.

Magnesium is involved in enzyme activation and is a component of chlorophyll.

Manganese is involved in enzyme activation and water splitting.

Iron is involved in redox changes, photosynthesis and respiration.

Molybdenum is involved in redox changes and nitrate reduction.

Copper is involved in redox changes, photosynthesis and respiration.

Zinc functions as an enzyme cofactor and activator.

Boron contributes to membrane activity and cell division.

Chlorine contributes to charge balance and water splitting.

Nickel is a component of some enzymes and contributes to biological nitrogen fixation and nitrogen metabolism.

## pH

Plant nutrition in hydroponics is closely associated with pH because pH affects nutrient availability.

The pH scale ranges from 0 to 14, with 7 neutral.

The scale is logarithmic, so small numerical changes represent large changes in hydrogen ion concentration.

The general optimal pH range for growing vegetables hydroponically is 5.0–7.0.

Nutrient availability varies with pH. For example, nitrogen is relatively more available around pH 6.0–7.5 and becomes less available at lower pH values.

## Water alkalinity

Alkalinity measures the capacity of water to neutralize acid.

It is primarily associated with carbonate and bicarbonate, although other compounds can contribute.

Low alkalinity means low buffering capacity, so water pH can change readily when substances are added.

High alkalinity is associated with higher water pH.

Acid can be injected with irrigation water to decrease high-pH water. The amount of acid required depends on the alkalinity of the water.

## Nutrient antagonism and interactions

Nutrients can interact with one another.

When one nutrient is present in excess, it can be taken up at a higher rate at the expense of another nutrient. This is known as nutrient antagonism.

A plant can therefore show a nutrient deficiency even when that nutrient is present in the nutrient solution.

Common nutrient antagonisms reported include:

Nitrogen — potassium

Phosphorus — zinc

Potassium — nitrogen, calcium, magnesium

Sodium — potassium, calcium, magnesium

Calcium — magnesium, boron

Magnesium — calcium

Iron — manganese

## Nutrition problems in hydroponics

Hydroponic systems are less forgiving than soil-based systems, and nutrient problems can result in plant symptoms quickly.

Regular monitoring of nutrient-solution composition and plant nutrient status is important.

Monitoring plants for visible symptoms can also help identify common nutrition problems.

## Soluble salts damage

### Cause

Soluble salt damage can result from over-fertilization, poor water quality, salt accumulation in aggregate media and inadequate leaching.

Fertilizers are salts and water loss through evaporation can increase soluble salt concentrations.

Irrigation water can also contain high levels of soluble salts.

### Symptoms

Excessive soluble salt levels can cause chemical-induced drought, resulting in plant wilting despite adequate irrigation.

Other symptoms include dark green foliage, dead or burned leaf margins and root death.

### Detection and management

Soluble salt levels can be monitored by measuring electrical conductivity (EC) of irrigation water, nutrient solutions and leachate.

Soluble salts can be leached with clear water after identifying and correcting the source of the high salt concentration.

## Nitrogen deficiency

### Cause

Nitrogen deficiency can result from under-fertilization, nutrient imbalance or excessive leaching.

### Symptoms

Typical symptoms include light green foliage and overall plant stunting.

Wilting and dead or yellow leaf margins can also occur.

### Detection and management

Monitoring the EC of nutrient solutions can help prevent nitrogen deficiency.

EC should be adjusted when it is outside the appropriate range.

The cause should be identified and corrected. This may involve increasing nitrogen in the nutrient solution or correcting an antagonistic nutrient excess.

## Calcium deficiency

### Cause

Calcium deficiency can result from under-fertilization, nutrient imbalance or low pH.

It is also associated with moisture management, high temperature and low airflow.

Calcium is transported through water-conducting tissues, so environmental conditions affecting water movement can influence calcium distribution.

### Symptoms

Symptoms can begin as brown margins on new growth.

Brown dead spots can develop as the problem progresses.

### Detection and management

Media should be monitored and plant analysis can be conducted.

The pH of nutrient solutions should be corrected to an appropriate range between 5.0 and 7.0.

Fertilizer may be applied when necessary.

In greenhouse environments, appropriate airflow can help manage transpiration and reduce calcium-related problems.

## Iron deficiency

### Cause

The most common cause of iron deficiency is high pH in the growing media or irrigation water.

Nutrient imbalance can also contribute.

### Symptoms

Iron deficiency appears as yellowing between leaf veins.

Symptoms generally appear first on new growth.

The article includes an example of hydroponic basil affected by severe iron deficiency.

### Detection and management

Media should be monitored and plant analysis can be conducted.

Correcting the pH of the nutrient solution is important.

Iron fertilizer may be applied when required.

## Magnesium deficiency

### Cause

Magnesium deficiency can be associated with high media pH or nutrient imbalance.

### Symptoms

Yellowing between leaf veins is a typical symptom.

Magnesium deficiency generally appears first on lower to middle leaves, which can help distinguish it from iron deficiency.

### Detection and management

Media should be monitored and plant analysis can be conducted.

Correct the pH of the nutrient solution.

## Boron toxicity

### Cause

Boron toxicity occurs when excessive boron is supplied.

Boron has a relatively narrow range between deficiency and toxicity, making over-application possible.

Boron may also be present in irrigation water, so water should be tested and its boron contribution considered when applying boron fertilizer.

### Symptoms

Boron toxicity can cause yellow and dead spots on leaf margins.

Reduced root growth can also occur.

### Detection and management

Media should be monitored and plant analysis can be conducted.

The source of excess boron should be identified and corrected.
