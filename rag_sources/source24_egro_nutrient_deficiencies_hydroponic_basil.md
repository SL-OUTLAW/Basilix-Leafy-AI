# Symptoms of Common Nutrient Deficiencies in Hydroponic Basil

Source ID: Source 24

Source: e-GRO / Cornell University research update

Priority: HIGH

URL: https://e-gro.org/pdf/2016-4.pdf

Topic: Hydroponic basil, nutrient deficiencies, visual diagnosis, nutrient management

Publication date: January 2016

## Overview

In hydroponic production, the fertilizer solution must provide all essential plant elements because a growing substrate is either absent or mainly provides physical support and access to water and oxygen.

Basil (Ocimum basilicum) is a commonly grown hydroponic herb crop.

Monitoring plants for visual symptoms can help detect nutrient deficiencies.

This research studied sweet basil 'Genovese' grown under nutrient solutions deficient in individual macro- and micronutrients to document the visual symptoms, timing and progression of nutrient deficiencies.

## Summary of findings

Deficiency symptoms of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium were observed primarily on mature leaves. In many cases, symptoms progressed over time to recently developed leaves.

Calcium, iron and boron deficiencies affected new growth and young leaves.

Sulfur deficiency caused uniform chlorosis across leaves, first becoming evident on new growth and eventually affecting the entire plant.

## Nitrogen deficiency

Nitrogen deficiency caused uniform chlorosis, or yellowing, of older leaves.

Symptoms were observed after approximately two weeks under deficient conditions.

Reduced plant size became evident compared with control plants, and mature leaves developed severe chlorosis after approximately three weeks.

## Phosphorus deficiency

Phosphorus deficiency initially appeared as numerous small purple spots on older leaves.

The symptoms became evident after approximately three weeks under deficient conditions.

Over time, purple regions on lower leaves became larger and interveinal chlorosis developed.

By week five, phosphorus-deficient plants had flowered earlier than control plants.

## Potassium deficiency

Potassium deficiency produced necrotic spots between the veins of the oldest leaves.

Symptoms appeared within approximately two weeks and became more pronounced after three weeks.

After approximately three to four weeks, large chlorotic and necrotic regions developed along leaf margins and in scattered areas between veins.

## Calcium deficiency

Calcium deficiency began with necrotic spots near the base of young leaves.

Symptoms appeared within approximately three days under deficient conditions.

Within one week, the growing point had died and additional necrotic spots had developed.

The root system also became noticeably brown.

## Magnesium deficiency

Magnesium deficiency initially appeared as faint interveinal chlorosis in recently mature middle leaves.

Symptoms were observed after approximately two weeks.

As deficiency progressed, interveinal chlorosis extended to younger leaves while older leaves developed necrotic regions between the veins.

## Sulfur deficiency

Sulfur deficiency caused uniform chlorosis across the entire leaf blade.

Within approximately two weeks, all leaves were affected.

The chlorosis became more pronounced over time and plant size was greatly reduced compared with control plants.

## Iron deficiency

Iron deficiency caused interveinal chlorosis of upper, young leaves while lower leaves remained green.

In the experiment, symptoms were not observed until approximately four weeks of iron-deficient conditions.

## Boron deficiency

Boron deficiency initially appeared as faint necrotic regions between the veins near the base of young leaves.

Symptoms were first noticeable after approximately three weeks.

As the deficiency progressed, the youngest leaves became distorted or strap-like and young leaves also developed interveinal chlorosis.

The root system became smaller than that of control plants, with short primary roots and many stubby lateral roots.

## Diagnosing nutrient disorders

Visual diagnosis is an important tool, but many nutrient disorders can have similar appearances.

Laboratory leaf tissue analysis is necessary to verify symptoms after a nutritional problem has occurred.

Periodic laboratory analysis of nutrient solution can provide a more proactive approach and help identify nutritional problems before they lead to economic losses.

Nutrient solution analysis can be used to modify the fertilizer regime and help maintain an adequate supply of nutrients.

The timing of symptom development can vary depending on environmental conditions.

Nutrient deficiency symptoms may also have environmental or biotic causes rather than being caused directly by a lack of nutrients in the fertilizer solution.

High pH above 6.5 can reduce the solubility of iron, manganese, boron and other nutrients and can therefore contribute to nutrient deficiencies.

Disease or insect damage can also resemble nutrient disorders, so plants should be examined carefully to determine the actual cause of symptoms.

## Control nutrient solution used in the experiment

The control nutrient solution used during the experimental period contained the following concentrations:

Nitrogen: 210 ppm

Phosphorus: 31 ppm

Potassium: 235 ppm

Calcium: 200 ppm

Magnesium: 49 ppm

Sulfur: 64 ppm

Iron: 4.0 ppm

Manganese: 0.5 ppm

Zinc: 0.1 ppm

Boron: 0.5 ppm

Copper: 0.10 ppm

Molybdenum: 0.01 ppm
