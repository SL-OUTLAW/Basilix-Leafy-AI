## Water and nutrient delivery

The general approach to nutrient-solution delivery is to apply the appropriate amount of solution at a frequency that maintains suitable moisture in the growing media while allowing a small amount of leaching during irrigation.

A small amount of leaching helps prevent salts from accumulating in the growing media and helps ensure containers receive enough solution to rewet the media.

Overwatering wastes nutrient solution and can create environmental concerns when excess leachate is not collected or reused.

Different growing media have different water-holding capacities, so irrigation schedules should be adapted to the production system.

Fine coconut fiber has a greater water-holding capacity than standard horticultural perlite or composted pine bark.

## Determining media water-holding capacity

Growers should determine the water-holding capacity of their soilless growing media.

A basic test involves weighing completely dry media, saturating the media with water, allowing drainage to finish, and weighing the saturated media.

The difference between dry and saturated weight represents the amount of water held by the media.

Dividing the water weight by 8.34 provides the approximate volume of water in gallons because one gallon of water weighs approximately 8.34 pounds.

Media with lower water-holding capacity generally requires shorter but more frequent irrigation events because it begins draining more quickly.

## Irrigation timing

Plants grown in most soilless media may require irrigation several times per day.

The number of irrigation events generally increases as plants grow.

Monitoring media moisture and the amount of leachate can help determine when irrigation frequency should be increased.

Irrigation should replace nutrient solution taken up by plants during the day.

Water uptake is associated with evapotranspiration, which includes plant transpiration and evaporation from the media.

More than 95% of water loss from the plant/media system may occur through transpiration.

Night-time irrigation is generally not needed in hydroponic vegetable production because transpiration is low at night.

Very early morning irrigation should also be avoided when transpiration rates are still low. Irrigation should generally begin after sunrise as greenhouse conditions warm and plant evapotranspiration increases.

## Irrigation and soluble salt management

Irrigation systems do not always distribute solution with perfect efficiency or uniformity, so some plants may receive too little solution if irrigation timing is based on lower-demand plants.

Excessive fertilizer concentration in the growing media can cause soluble salt burn.

Soluble salt burn damages roots through dehydration and can produce marginal leaf necrosis and slight plant wilting.

High soluble salt concentrations reduce the ability of roots to take up water.

## Nutrient-solution concentration

Correct nutrient-solution formulation is important for preventing soluble salt damage.

Electrical conductivity (EC) of the nutrient solution delivered to plants should be monitored as an indicator that the solution has been formulated correctly.

In the Florida greenhouse systems described in the guide, final nutrient-solution EC is commonly in the range of 1.5–3.5 dS/m.

Young crops generally start at the lower end of the EC range, with EC gradually increased as the crop matures.

The appropriate EC depends on the crop, environmental conditions and production system.

## Monitoring leachate EC

EC of the leachate from growing media can be used to determine whether nutrient concentrations are building up to damaging levels.

In soilless media systems, leachate EC should generally remain within approximately 1.0 EC unit above or below the EC of the applied nutrient solution.

If leachate EC is more than 1.0 unit lower than the applied nutrient solution, insufficient nutrients may be reaching the final solution delivered to the crop.

If leachate EC is more than 1.0 unit higher than the incoming nutrient solution, the nutrient-solution concentration may be too high.

High leachate EC can also indicate inadequate irrigation because the crop is using water and salts are accumulating.

If leachate EC is slightly high, the grower can adjust the nutrient-solution concentration by changing fertilizer proportions or diluting the stock solution.

If leachate EC is excessively high, the growing media can be flushed with water.

After flushing, the grower should return to a properly adjusted nutrient solution rather than continuing to irrigate only with water for an extended period.

## Leachate collection

Leachate collection systems can be simple and inexpensive.

A collection system should be placed at representative plant locations within the growing area.

Leachate should be collected from healthy plants.

Containers should not sit in collected leachate because prolonged contact can saturate the growing media and cause poor growth or plant death.

Growers should adjust irrigation frequency and duration based on weather and crop conditions.

Monitoring leachate can help refine nutrient programs and prevent wasteful over-irrigation and over-fertilization.

## Irrigation and leachate measurements

Growers should monitor both the volume of irrigation solution delivered to the crop and the volume of leachate leaving the media.

These measurements provide data for developing an irrigation plan based on actual water use.

Irrigation volume should be measured and recorded regularly.

The data can be used to identify changes in crop water requirements and refine water and nutrient-management plans over time.

For testing purposes, the recommended leachate volume is approximately 10–20% of the total daily irrigation volume applied.

## Leachate reuse

Collecting and reusing leachate can reduce waste.

Small operations may avoid direct recirculation because the leachate may need to be sanitized and its nutrients replenished before reuse.

Collected solution can also be used for other appropriate purposes such as irrigating other crops or landscaped areas where suitable.

## Automated irrigation control

Irrigation systems can be automated to different degrees.

A simple system can use a timer to turn irrigation on and off at preset intervals.

More advanced irrigation controllers can control fertigation timing, frequency and other inputs such as accumulated sunlight, temperature and changes in media weight.

Greater automation can improve nutrient-solution mixing and delivery capabilities but also increases costs.

Larger operations may benefit from higher levels of automation because reduced labour and more efficient input use can offset the cost.

Small growers may benefit more from simpler systems.

## Moisture sensors

Soilless-media moisture sensors can measure volumetric water content and provide information for improving irrigation scheduling.

Capacitance-based moisture sensors can respond to changes in media moisture.

Initial research described in the guide suggested that these sensors were compatible with fine coconut-fiber media but less suitable for coarse media such as perlite or composted pine bark.

Moisture sensors, together with leachate monitoring, can provide growers with useful information for reducing unnecessary use of water, nutrients and labour.
